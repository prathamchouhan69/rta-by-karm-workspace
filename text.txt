1. INSPECT EVERY REFERENCE IN DEPTH

Go deeply into every supplied video, image, document, design file, and other reference.

Do not skim, rely on thumbnails, or make assumptions from a few screenshots.

For videos:
• Inspect every scene, transition, interaction, and meaningful change.
• Use frame-by-frame inspection wherever your tools support it.
• Examine the beginning, middle, and end of each motion sequence.
• Record timestamps for the specific behaviours you intend to reproduce.
• Study geometry, depth, composition, materials, lighting, shadows, reflections, textures, layering, typography, iconography, and data representation.
• Study how elements enter, move, transform, react, connect, and exit.
• Examine timing, duration, delay, easing, acceleration, deceleration, overshoot, spring behaviour, staggering, looping, and synchronization.
• Distinguish camera movement from object movement and scene transitions.
• Inspect hover, selection, active, expanded, collapsed, loading, empty, success, warning, error, and disabled states whenever shown.

For images and design files:
• Inspect every visible component and its relationship to the overall composition.
• Study spacing, proportions, alignment, hierarchy, geometry, depth, surface treatment, and visual density.
• Inspect small details as carefully as major visual elements.

For documents:
• Read every accessible page, section, table, diagram, annotation, and relevant requirement.
• Extract functional requirements separately from visual inspiration.
• Preserve accounting rules, business logic, data relationships, and technical constraints.

Treat “atom-by-atom analysis” as a demand for exhaustive practical inspection—not permission to claim access to details you cannot actually inspect.

If your tools cannot open a file, inspect video frames, play a clip, or identify a motion reliably, state that limitation clearly. Do not claim to have reviewed it.
